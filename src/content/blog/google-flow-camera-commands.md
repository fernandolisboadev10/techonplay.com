Google Flow just got a lot more fun to use, if you know the right words to type. **Google Flow camera commands** are short prompt tags that tell the AI exactly how to frame a shot, from a drone view straight down to a tight close-up on your face.

Most people typing into Flow stick to basic prompts like “make a cool photo of me.” That gets generic results. The creators pulling millions of views right now are typing specific camera and drone commands instead, and the difference in output quality is obvious the moment you compare the two.

This guide breaks down all 40 commands, grouped by what they actually do, plus a comparison table so you can find the right one in seconds. Grab a photo of yourself, pick a command below, and start generating.

## What Google Flow Camera Commands Actually Do

**Read this first:** the `/COMMAND` tags in this list are community prompt keywords that circulated on social media. Google does not document a slash-command syntax for Flow, and its Help Center lists keyboard shortcuts, not prompt tags. Flow does not parse them as special switches. Treat each tag as a compact way of telling the model "low angle," "orbit," or "close-up," which it interprets like any other description. If a tag has no visible effect, write the shot out in plain words (for example, "low-angle shot looking up at the subject") and the result is usually the same or better.

Camera prompts work like a director’s shot list, translated into text. Instead of describing a scene in vague terms, you tell Flow the exact angle, distance, and movement you want. Think of it as the difference between telling a photographer “take a nice picture” versus “shoot this from a low angle, close up, with a shallow depth of field.” One gets you a random result. The other gets you a specific look.

These commands were first popularized through social media carousels showing them applied to a well known athlete. The same structure works for anyone, including a normal selfie shot in your living room.

## Aerial and Angle Commands: From Drone Shots to Ground Level

This first group controls the vertical position of the camera relative to you. These are the commands that turn a flat photo into something that looks like it was shot by a production crew.

✅ **Best for:** hero shots, profile pictures, anything that needs to feel bigger than a phone selfie ❌ **Avoid for:** casual, everyday posts where a dramatic angle feels out of place

-   `/DRONEVIEW`: High aerial view from above
-   `/TOPDOWN`: Directly above the subject
-   `/BIRDSEYE`: Bird’s-eye perspective
-   `/OVERHEAD`: Camera directly overhead
-   `/EXTREMETOPDOWN`: Ultra-high aerial view straight down
-   `/DRONE3Q`: High drone view at an angle
-   `/LOWANGLE`: Camera looking upward, makes the subject feel powerful
-   `/HIGHANGLE`: Camera looking downward
-   `/GROUNDLEVEL`: Camera close to the ground
-   `/WORMSVIEW`: Extremely low-angle perspective, dramatic and unusual
-   `/LOWUP`: Low-angle close-up built for a strong, confident look

📊 **Data point:** angle-based prompts are the single biggest lever for making an AI-generated photo feel professional instead of AI-generated. A slight low angle alone can change how authoritative a portrait looks.

## Camera Movement Commands That Add Cinematic Motion

Static shots are fine, but movement commands are what make people stop scrolling. These simulate a camera physically moving through space, which Flow renders as motion blur, depth shifts, or a tracking feel.

⏱️ These work especially well for short video generations, not just single images.

-   `/TRACKING`: Cinematic moving-camera feel
-   `/ORBIT`: Camera circling the subject
-   `/ORBITCLOSE`: Camera circling closer around the subject
-   `/PUSHIN`: Camera moving toward the subject
-   `/PULLOUT`: Camera moving away from the subject
-   `/CRANEUP`: Low to high rising shot
-   `/DRONEREVEAL`: Camera pulls back to reveal the full scene
-   `/MOTIONBLUR`: Subject in motion with a blur effect
-   `/AERIALTRACK`: Drone tracking the subject from above

If you only try three commands from this whole list, make it `/PUSHIN`, `/ORBIT`, and `/DRONEREVEAL`. They consistently produce the most shareable results.

## Framing and Composition Commands for Better Storytelling

This group is about distance and composition rather than angle. These are the commands that separate a snapshot from something that looks intentional.

-   `/CLOSEUP`: Face-focused close-up
-   `/MEDIUMSHOT`: Upper-body or half-body shot
-   `/FULLBODY`: Full subject visible
-   `/PORTRAIT`: Portrait-focused framing
-   `/WIDEANGLE`: Wide environmental shot
-   `/LONGSHOT`: Subject small, environment large
-   `/PROFILE`: Side profile shot
-   `/CLOSEPROFILE`: Tight profile close-up
-   `/OVERSHOULDER`: Shot from behind the shoulder toward the subject
-   `/POV`: Subject looking directly into the camera
-   `/BEHIND`: Camera positioned behind the subject
-   `/EYELEVEL`: Natural, eye-level shot with no distortion

## Mood and Effect Commands for a Cinematic Finish

The last group adds atmosphere. These commands do not change where the camera sits, they change how the shot feels once it is framed.

-   `/CINEMATIC`: Adds cinematic composition and depth
-   `/DUTCHANGLE`: Dramatic tilted camera
-   `/DUTCHTILT`: Tilted camera for a similar dramatic effect
-   `/SILHOUETTE`: Backlit silhouette shot
-   `/REFLECTION`: Shot including a natural reflection
-   `/FRAMED`: Subject framed by the surrounding environment
-   `/THROUGHOBJECT`: Shot through a foreground element
-   `/THROUGHNET`: Shot through a net, fence, or similar frame

## How to Actually Use These Commands With Your Own Photos

Using these prompts is simple once you understand the pattern. Upload a clear photo of yourself, then add one command tag to your text prompt, describing the setting you want.

For example: “A person standing in a modern kitchen, /LOWANGLE, natural lighting, casual outfit.” The model usually treats the tag as a camera-angle hint, but results vary, so add a plain-language description too ("low angle, looking up at the subject").

You can combine two commands for a more specific result, such as pairing `/PORTRAIT` with `/CINEMATIC` for a magazine-style headshot. Do not stack more than two or three, since the model can start ignoring conflicting instructions.

A quick note on faces: since you are recreating these shots with your own photo instead of a public figure, results tend to be more consistent and there is no risk of the output resembling someone else’s likeness.

## Common Mistakes When Prompting Camera Angles in Google Flow

-   ❌ Typing the command without describing the scene around it, which produces a floating, generic subject
-   ❌ Combining conflicting angles, like `/TOPDOWN` and `/EYELEVEL` in the same prompt
-   ❌ Using a blurry or low-resolution source photo, which limits how sharp the final angle looks
-   ✅ Pairing one angle command with one mood command for the cleanest results
-   ✅ Testing the same prompt twice, since Flow varies slightly between generations

## FAQ

**Do I need a paid Google Flow plan to use these commands?**

_These tags are ordinary prompt text, so no special plan is needed just to type them. What you can generate (models, limits, resolution) depends on your Google AI plan; check the current plan details on Google's [Gemini subscriptions page](https://gemini.google/subscriptions/) before relying on a given feature. Flow also has its own built-in camera controls for moving and angling shots, which are separate from these tags._

**Can I use more than one camera command in the same prompt?**

_Yes, combining two works well, such as an angle command with a mood command. Adding three or more often confuses the output, so keep it simple for the most reliable results._

**Do these commands work for video generation too?**

_Yes. Movement commands like `/ORBIT`, `/PUSHIN`, and `/DRONEREVEAL` are actually built for motion and tend to look better in video generations than in single static images._

**Will using my own face instead of a celebrity change the output quality?**

_No, the commands control the camera behavior, not the subject. A clear, well-lit source photo of yourself works just as well as any other subject._

**Where did this list of commands originally come from?**

_The format was popularized through social media posts using a well known athlete as the visual example. Google has not published them as official Flow commands; they are generic prompt keywords that work (to varying degrees) with any subject._
