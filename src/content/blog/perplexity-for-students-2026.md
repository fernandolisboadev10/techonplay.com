## Turn On Academic Focus Before You Start a Literature Review

Most students never touch Perplexity’s Focus modes, which is a mistake. The default search pulls from the open web, mixing blog posts, forums, and news alongside anything scholarly.

Switch to **Academic Focus** and Perplexity restricts results to peer-reviewed journals, preprint servers like arXiv and bioRxiv, conference proceedings, and academic databases such as Semantic Scholar and PubMed. For a literature review or a research paper with citation requirements, this single toggle saves you from digging through SEO blog spam to find an actual source.

### A workflow that works for papers

1.  Open a new thread and switch the Focus selector to Academic.
2.  Ask your research question directly: “What does recent research say about \[your topic\]?”
3.  Follow up on specific results: “What methodology did \[author\] use in that study?”
4.  Pull up the original source before you cite anything. Perplexity’s job is to point you to the paper, not to replace reading it.

📊 Treat Perplexity as a search layer, not a source. Cite the paper it found, never the Perplexity answer itself. Professors and citation managers alike expect the primary source.

## Organize Every Class in Its Own Space

Spaces are Perplexity’s answer to the “wait, what was I even researching last week” problem. Each Space is a persistent thread that keeps its own history, uploaded files, and context, so you can close your laptop and pick the same project back up a week later without re-explaining anything.

The setup that works best for a full semester:

✅ One Space per class or per major assignment, not one giant catch-all thread ✅ Name it something you will recognize later, like “Bio 201 Midterm Paper” instead of “Untitled” ✅ Upload the syllabus or assigned readings directly into the Space so answers stay grounded in your actual course material ✅ Pin the sources you already verified so you are not re-checking the same citation twice

## Write Prompts That Actually Save You Time

Vague prompts get vague answers, even from a tool built around sourcing. A few prompt patterns consistently save the most time on real coursework:

**For a literature review:** “Summarize the last five years of peer-reviewed research on \[topic\]. Group findings by theme and flag where studies disagree.”

**For an essay outline:** “I am arguing that \[your thesis\]. Find three sources that support this and one strong counterargument I need to address.”

**For exam prep:** “Explain \[concept\] the way a professor would for an undergraduate exam, then give me three practice questions with answers.”

Each of these gives Perplexity a job, a scope, and an output format, which is the difference between a one-line answer and something you can actually drop into a draft.

## The Mistake That Gets Students in Trouble

The single most common misuse is citing Perplexity itself in a bibliography instead of the primary source it points to. Perplexity is a research assistant that finds and summarizes papers, not a publisher. Always click through to the original article, journal, or dataset and cite that directly. Teachers face the same questions, as covered in [AI for education](/ai-for-education-2026/).

The second mistake is trusting a single AI-generated summary without cross-checking it. Ask the same research question in Academic Focus and a general model, or ask Perplexity to find contradicting evidence on purpose. If two searches agree, you can move faster. If they do not, that disagreement is usually worth digging into before you write it down as fact.

### Is Perplexity better than ChatGPT for schoolwork?

_It depends on the task. Perplexity is stronger for anything that needs a citation, since every answer links back to a real source. ChatGPT is generally faster for brainstorming, drafting, and rewriting where sourcing is not the point. Many students end up using both for different parts of the same assignment._

### Can professors tell if I used Perplexity?

_Perplexity itself is not a plagiarism tool and does not flag anything. The risk is submitting an unedited AI summary as your own writing, or citing Perplexity instead of the primary source, both of which are easy for an instructor to spot. Use it for research, then write the final draft yourself._

### Do I need Comet, or just the Perplexity app?

_The free Comet browser is a nice-to-have, not a requirement. Everything described in this guide works through Perplexity’s regular web app or mobile app. Comet mainly saves time on page summaries and fact-checking while you browse, which matters most during heavy reading weeks._
